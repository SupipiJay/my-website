PROJECT OVERVIEW

Making supportive oral care more accessible

Oral conditions can affect health, communication, nutrition, and quality of life. The project explores how AI-assisted analysis and patient support features can help people recognise concerns earlier and seek appropriate professional advice.

DOMAIN

Research domain

The completed research system sits at the intersection of artificial intelligence, medical image analysis, machine learning, speech signal processing, digital health, and human-centred supportive care.

Research problem

Oral conditions may be difficult to identify early when assessment depends on subjective visual examination, specialist availability, and access to care. Existing digital tools often focus on one condition or one-time screening and may not integrate ongoing monitoring, speech changes, psychological wellbeing, and caregiver support.

Research gap

The completed framework addresses the gap between single-purpose oral screening tools and a more integrated, patient-centred support system. It combines multiple complementary components while deliberately avoiding autonomous medical diagnosis.

Main objective

To develop a holistic oral-health mobile system integrating AI-based oral condition analysis, dual-stage risk assessment, psychological support through guided meditation, speech therapy monitoring, and guardian-assisted alerts to encourage timely clinical intervention.

Methodology overview

  1. Collect inputsOral images, relevant risk-factor information, voice samples, and user-reported wellbeing data.
  2. Process and assessApply image analysis, risk scoring, voice/speech feature analysis, and supportive wellbeing logic.
  3. Present outputsProvide explainable indicators, progress reports, care guidance, and appropriate alerts.
  4. Evaluate responsiblyAssess performance with suitable data, domain expertise, privacy controls, and required ethical approval.

Research significance

The framework brings together four complementary capabilities in one patient-support experience: visual oral-condition review, risk-factor assessment, voice and speech monitoring, and psychological wellbeing support. This integrated approach makes information easier to understand, supports follow-up over time, and helps users prepare for conversations with qualified professionals.

Research questions

  • How can AI-assisted image analysis present oral visual indicators in a clear and responsible way?
  • How can lifestyle factors and reported symptoms be combined into understandable risk summaries and follow-up trends?
  • Which speech and voice features can be tracked consistently across recordings?
  • How can wellbeing support and guardian alerts be designed around consent, privacy, and safe escalation?

Scope and intended users

The system is designed for people seeking supportive oral-health information, caregivers where the user has authorised their involvement, and researchers evaluating the integrated workflow. Its scope is screening support, monitoring, education, and wellbeing—not diagnosis, treatment prescription, or a replacement for clinical assessment.

Privacy, ethics, and limitations

Oral images, health-related answers, voice recordings, and wellbeing check-ins are sensitive information. Collection should be limited to what is necessary, explained clearly, protected with appropriate access controls, and handled with informed consent and relevant institutional ethics approval. Model performance may vary with image quality, recording conditions, data representation, and individual differences; outputs must be communicated with these limitations in mind.

RESEARCH APPROACH

Research Methodology

A structured, component-based methodology connects user needs, data processing, AI-assisted analysis, supportive outputs, and evaluation into one integrated framework.

✦
Methodological approach

Applied system development with modular implementation and evaluation. Each component addresses a defined research objective, while shared interface and data services support a consistent user experience.

◎
Guiding principles

Human-centred design, privacy by design, explainable outputs, consent for sensitive data, and clear boundaries between supportive screening and professional diagnosis.

01⌕

Problem definition & requirements

Define the research problem and the needs of users, caregivers, and researchers. Translate these needs into functional requirements for image analysis, risk monitoring, voice assessment, wellbeing support, reporting, and access control.

  • Map each research objective to a component.
  • Identify expected inputs, outputs, and user journeys.
  • Record safety, accessibility, and privacy requirements.
02▧

Data and input preparation

Define the information each module needs and prepare it for processing. Image and audio inputs require quality checks; risk and wellbeing inputs require understandable questions and carefully defined data fields.

  • Check file format, completeness, and quality.
  • Use consent and data minimisation for sensitive inputs.
  • Separate identifying details from analysis data where appropriate.
03⚙

Component implementation

Develop each module independently, with clearly defined inputs, processing steps, and outputs. Use suitable image-processing and vision methods, risk scoring, audio feature extraction, and wellbeing support logic.

  • Image module: prepare images and assess visual patterns.
  • Risk module: combine risk factors, symptoms, and follow-up history.
  • Voice and wellbeing modules: analyse trends and provide supportive outputs.
04⇄

Integration & data management

Connect the modules through the application interface and backend API. Standardise response formats so users can navigate between assessments, view previous results, and understand recommendations consistently.

  • Validate requests and handle errors clearly.
  • Store only necessary records with access controls.
  • Keep module boundaries clear for maintenance and testing.
05✓

Testing & evaluation

Evaluate both individual modules and the integrated user journey. Evaluation measures should match the type of output and the available validated data; results should be reported honestly, including uncertainty and limitations.

  • Test normal, invalid, incomplete, and poor-quality inputs.
  • Assess suitable performance, usability, and consistency measures.
  • Check that errors, warnings, and guidance are understandable.
06♧

Responsible use & refinement

Review the system for safe communication, privacy, accessibility, and appropriate escalation. Use evaluation findings to refine the interface, processing pipeline, and explanations without overstating clinical capability.

  • Explain that outputs are supportive, not definitive diagnoses.
  • Require consent before guardian alerts or sensitive sharing.
  • Document limitations and areas requiring further validation.
Methodology outcome

An integrated research framework in which four specialised components provide understandable analysis and supportive follow-up through a shared application experience. The validity of each AI output depends on the data, model, and evaluation evidence available.

FOUR COMPONENTS

One framework, four areas of support

Each component has a focused role and contributes to a connected patient-support workflow.

◉VISION
IMAGE ANALYSIS

Oral Condition Image Analysis & Care Guidance

Users submit oral images for AI-assisted analysis. The system identifies visual indicators such as colour changes, patches, ulcers, or irregular tissue patterns, assigns colour-coded concern levels, recommends professional consultation when appropriate, and provides supportive care guidance.

Typical inputClear oral-cavity photo uploaded by the user
Expected outputVisual indicators, an understandable concern level, and next-step guidance
Research evaluationImage quality, classification performance, sensitivity, and limitations
System workflow
  1. Accept an image and check basic quality.
  2. Preprocess the image and run the selected vision model.
  3. Show an explainable, colour-coded concern category.
  4. Offer condition-aligned self-care information and recommend clinical review when indicated.

Contribution: connects image-based screening support with understandable guidance and advice to seek professional assessment. The design intentionally avoids presenting an AI result as a confirmed oral-cancer diagnosis.

Processing details & user experience

The image pathway should guide the user to capture a well-lit, in-focus image and explain when an image is unsuitable for assessment. The processing sequence can include image validation, resizing or normalisation, model inference, and conversion of the model output into a plain-language summary.

Input quality checkImage preprocessingModel inferencePlain-language result

Responsible output: describe the result as an AI-generated indicator, show uncertainty where available, and encourage professional assessment for persistent, worsening, or concerning symptoms.

⌁RISK AI
RISK & PROGRESSION

Multimodal Oral Cancer Risk Assessment & Monitoring

Core risk uses relevant predictors such as age, smoking, alcohol use, betel chewing, nutrition, and oral hygiene. Extended risk adds symptoms and indicators such as ulcers, pain, chewing or swallowing difficulty, and changes in voice. Repeated assessments show trends over time and support timely medical review.

Typical inputRisk factors, reported symptoms, and follow-up responses
Expected outputCore and extended risk summaries, trends, and review prompts
Research evaluationRisk-model performance, explainability, and consistency over time
System workflow
  1. Collect demographic and lifestyle factors to estimate a baseline or core risk.
  2. Add oral-health indicators and symptoms to form an extended risk summary.
  3. Repeat assessments at planned intervals and visualise changes in a trend view.
  4. Provide an alert or clinical-review prompt when predefined thresholds are reached.

Contribution: combines several kinds of information and repeat assessments to support earlier conversations with qualified healthcare professionals; it does not determine whether a person has cancer.

Risk dimensions & longitudinal monitoring

The core assessment summarises established lifestyle and demographic inputs; the extended assessment adds reported oral symptoms and other relevant indicators. Keeping the two views distinct helps users understand which information contributes to each summary. A dated assessment history supports comparison across follow-ups.

Core risk factorsSymptom indicatorsFollow-up historyTrend comparison

Responsible output: explain that a risk category is not a diagnosis and that a low score cannot rule out disease. Thresholds and predictive performance should only be claimed when supported by appropriate validation.

〰VOICE
SPEECH & VOICE

Speech Therapy and Voice Analysis

Voice recordings can be compared with a user's baseline and earlier samples. Potential features include articulation, clarity, pitch, jitter, shimmer, MFCCs, hoarseness, and consistency. Progress reports help patients and therapists observe changes and plan follow-up.

Typical inputShort voice recordings collected with user consent
Expected outputSpeech-feature summaries and comparisons with earlier samples
Research evaluationRecording quality, feature reliability, and progress-tracking usefulness
System workflow
  1. Record a baseline sample using a consistent prompt and with consent.
  2. Preprocess recordings and extract features such as pitch, jitter, shimmer, MFCCs, and voice-quality measures.
  3. Compare new recordings with baseline and previous samples.
  4. Summarise changes for discussion with a clinician or speech-language therapist.

Contribution: supports repeated monitoring and discussion with a speech therapist or clinician. Voice features can be affected by many factors and are not diagnostic on their own.

Audio processing & progress comparison

For useful comparisons, recordings should use a consistent prompt, similar recording distance, and reasonably quiet surroundings. The audio pipeline may check duration and clipping, reduce irrelevant noise where appropriate, extract acoustic features, and compare the new sample with prior samples.

Consistent recordingAudio quality checksAcoustic featuresBaseline comparison

Responsible output: report observed changes rather than making a diagnosis. Recording conditions, illness, fatigue, language, and individual variation may influence acoustic measurements.

✦WELLBEING
WELLBEING SUPPORT

Psychological Well-being, Meditation & Guardian Alerts

Self-reported mood, sleep, and selected behavioural signals can help estimate support needs. The component offers personalised meditation, breathing, relaxation, and sound-based support. Critical concerns may trigger an alert to a registered guardian according to the system's safety design.

Typical inputVoluntary mood and wellbeing check-ins
Expected outputGuided breathing or meditation resources and carefully designed support alerts
Research evaluationUsability, privacy, accessibility, and safe escalation design
System workflow
  1. Invite voluntary check-ins about mood, sleep, and wellbeing.
  2. Use the agreed rules or validated model to identify possible support needs.
  3. Offer appropriate guided breathing, meditation, or relaxation resources.
  4. Where consent and safety rules allow, notify a registered guardian for critical concerns and explain the limits of the service.

Contribution: adds a human-centred support layer. Guardian notifications should use clear consent, privacy safeguards, and a defined safety process; the feature is not a mental-health diagnosis.

Personalised support & safe escalation

The support pathway can match user-selected needs to breathing exercises, meditation, relaxation audio, or wellbeing resources. It should make the purpose of each check-in clear, allow users to skip non-essential questions, and provide simple controls for notification preferences.

Voluntary check-inSupport recommendationConsent settingsGuardian alert rules

Responsible output: explain what information may be shared, with whom, and under what circumstances. Alerts should follow a defined safeguarding process and should not be presented as a substitute for emergency or professional mental-health support.
